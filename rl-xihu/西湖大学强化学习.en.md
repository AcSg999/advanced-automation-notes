## Lecture 1. The grid-world example

### States, actions, transitions, and policies

A **state** describes the agent's situation relative to its environment. The **state space** $\mathcal S$ contains all possible states. At state $s$, the available actions form $\mathcal A(s)$.

The illustrated grid world uses five actions: up, right, down, left, and staying in place. A transition model $p(s'\mid s,a)$ specifies the probability of reaching state $s'$ after taking action $a$ at $s$. For a deterministic rightward move from $s_1$ to $s_2$, $p(s_2\mid s_1,a_{\mathrm{right}})=1$ and the other next-state probabilities are zero.

A **policy** $\pi(a\mid s)$ specifies how actions are selected. A deterministic policy assigns probability $1$ to one action. A stochastic policy can assign, for example, probability $0.5$ each to moving right and moving down.

A **reward** is a real-valued signal received after acting. Rewards can also be random, with distribution $p(r\mid s,a)$.

### Trajectories, returns, and the Markov property

Let $S_t$ be the state at time $t$, $A_t$ the chosen action, and $R_{t+1}$ the reward received after that action. A trajectory is a sequence of states, actions, and rewards. An episode is a finite trajectory ending in a terminal state.

Let $\gamma\in[0,1)$ be the discount factor. The discounted return from time $t$ is

$$
G_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma^jR_{t+j+1}.
$$

For a finite episode, the sum stops at termination. The **Markov property** says that the present state and action provide the information needed to predict the next transition: conditioning additionally on earlier history does not change the transition distribution.

An **MDP**, or Markov decision process, combines the state and action spaces, transition and reward models, and a return criterion. A policy completes the description of how the agent behaves within that process.

## Lecture 2. The Bellman equation

### State value and action value

The state-value function is the expected return under a fixed policy:

$$
v_\pi(s)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s].
$$

Many trajectories can begin at the same state. State value averages their returns using their probabilities. The subscript $\pi$ identifies the policy followed after starting at that state.

The Chinese comparison diagram emphasizes that $v_{\pi_1}(s)$ and $v_{\pi_2}(s)$ use the **same starting state** and different subsequent policies. A larger value means a larger expected return under the specified policy. Policy improvement is the separate operation that changes how actions are chosen.

Action value fixes the first action as well:

$$
q_\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s,A_t=a],
\qquad v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)q_\pi(s,a).
$$

Both quantities concern cumulative return. The one-step reward is only the first contribution to that return.

### Bootstrapping and the recursive equation

Use $G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}$. Let $r(s,a)=\mathbb E[R_{t+1}\mid S_t=s,A_t=a]$. Then

$$
v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)
\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v_\pi(s')\right].
$$

**Bootstrapping** means using an estimate of a future value to help estimate the current value. The Bellman equation links values of neighboring states through expected immediate rewards and future values.

For the deterministic four-state cycle in the slides, the equations have the pattern $v_1=r_1+\gamma v_2$, $v_2=r_2+\gamma v_3$, $v_3=r_3+\gamma v_4$, and $v_4=r_4+\gamma v_1$.

### Matrix-vector form and policy evaluation

For finitely many states, collect state values into $v_\pi$, expected one-step rewards into $r_\pi$, and policy-induced transition probabilities into $P_\pi$:

$$
r_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)r(s,a),
\qquad
P_\pi(s,s')=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)p(s'\mid s,a).
$$

The Bellman equation becomes

$$
v_\pi=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi v_\pi,
\qquad v_\pi=(I-\gamma P_\pi)^{-1}r_\pi.
$$

Finding the values for a given policy is **policy evaluation**. An iterative alternative to matrix inversion is

$$
V_{k+1}=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi V_k.
$$

Here $k$ counts iterations and $V_k$ is the entire current estimate vector. It is distinct from a state index or a policy subscript.

## Lecture 3. The Bellman optimality equation

### Comparing policies

A policy $\pi_1$ is at least as good as $\pi_2$ when $v_{\pi_1}(s)\geq v_{\pi_2}(s)$ for every state. An optimal policy achieves the optimal value $v_*$.

Replacing policy-weighted action averaging with maximization gives

$$
v_*(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}
\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v_*(s')\right].
$$

The original derivation first maximizes $\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)q(s,a)$ over action probabilities. A weighted average is maximized by placing all probability on an action with the largest $q(s,a)$.

In the slide example, $q_1,q_2,q_3$ denote values of three actions at one state. They are entries of an action-value function, rather than three independently defined value functions.

### Fixed points and contractions

Write the optimality backup as $V_{k+1}=\mathcal T_*V_k$. A **fixed point** satisfies $V=\mathcal T_*V$. In a discounted finite MDP, this operator is a contraction in the maximum norm:

$$
\|\mathcal T_*V-\mathcal T_*U\|_\infty
\leq\gamma\|V-U\|_\infty.
$$

Since $\gamma<1$, successive backups converge to a unique fixed point. A deterministic greedy policy can then choose

$$
a_*(s)\in\arg\max_a q_*(s,a),
\qquad \pi_*(a\mid s)=\mathbf1\{a=a_*(s)\}.
$$

The known inputs are rewards, transition probabilities, and the discount factor. The optimal values and policy are the quantities to solve for.

The source also asks what happens when a reward is transformed to $ar+b$. For continuing discounted tasks with a common horizon convention and $a>0$, values transform as $v'_\pi=av_\pi+b/(1-\gamma)$, preserving policy ordering. Termination and reward timing must be specified when applying this idea to episodic tasks.

## Lecture 4. Value iteration and policy iteration

### Value iteration

Start with an arbitrary estimate $V_0$ and repeat

$$
V_{k+1}(s)=\max_a\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)V_k(s')\right].
$$

It can be viewed as choosing a greedy action using $V_k$, then performing one value backup. Stop when successive estimates are sufficiently close. Intermediate $V_k$ vectors are iterative approximations; an intermediate vector need not be the exact value of a particular policy.

### Policy iteration

Initialize $\pi_0$, then alternate:

1. **Policy evaluation:** compute $v_{\pi_k}$, for example with an inner iteration $V^{(j+1)}=r_{\pi_k}+\gamma P_{\pi_k}V^{(j)}$.
2. **Policy improvement:** choose actions greedy with respect to $v_{\pi_k}$.

The policy improvement guarantee is $v_{\pi_{k+1}}(s)\geq v_{\pi_k}(s)$ for every state. Equality is possible, including at an optimum.

The slide's four questions are how evaluation is computed, why improvement works, why repeated improvement reaches an optimum in the finite setting, and how this procedure relates to value iteration. The inner evaluation loop explains why policy iteration contains another iteration inside each outer iteration.

![Successive policies and value estimates in the grid-world policy-iteration example](images/037.png)

The example uses boundary reward $-1$, forbidden-cell reward $-10$, target reward $1$, and $\gamma=0.9$. A Chinese explanatory panel considers a yellow cell with current value $10$. Moving right into it with reward $-10$ gives the backup

$$
Q(s,a_{\mathrm{right}})=-10+0.9\times10=-1.
$$

This shows why an action must be judged by both its immediate reward and its next-state value. Another ordinary move in that example may also have a one-step cost of $-1$.

### Truncated policy iteration

Full policy evaluation converges before each policy improvement. **Truncated policy iteration** performs only a finite number of evaluation backups. One backup connects it to value iteration; increasing the number moves it toward full policy iteration.

## Lecture 5. Monte Carlo learning

### From a model to sampled returns

Value and policy iteration use the environment model. Monte Carlo, MC, evaluation estimates values from sampled episodes. The law of large numbers motivates replacing an expectation by a sample average; for independent samples with finite variance, the sample mean's variance is the single-sample variance divided by the sample count.

For a fixed starting pair $(s,a)$, execute $a$ and then follow $\pi$ to generate returns $G^{(1)},\ldots,G^{(N)}$. Estimate

$$
q_\pi(s,a)\approx\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}G^{(i)}.
$$

The policy may be deterministic while transitions and rewards remain random. Experience, data, and samples refer to the observed interaction outcomes.

The basic MC control procedure evaluates actions with sampled returns, improves the policy greedily, and collects further episodes. The source's episode-length examples illustrate that truncating trajectories changes the returns used for evaluation.

### Visits and exploration

A visit is an occurrence of a state-action pair in an episode.

- **Initial-visit estimation** uses returns from the selected initial pair.
- **First-visit estimation** uses the return following the first occurrence of a pair in each episode.
- **Every-visit estimation** uses the return after every occurrence of that pair.

**Exploring starts** assumes every state-action pair can be selected as a starting pair with positive probability. This can be difficult to arrange in a physical environment.

A **soft policy** gives each available action positive probability. An $\epsilon$-greedy policy selects a greedy action most of the time and explores otherwise. Let $m=|\mathcal A(s)|$, with one selected greedy action $a^*$:

$$
\pi(a\mid s)=
\begin{cases}
1-\epsilon+\epsilon/m,&a=a^*,\\
\epsilon/m,&a\neq a^*.
\end{cases}
$$

Exploitation uses the action currently estimated to be best; exploration gathers information about alternatives. The notes suggest starting with greater exploration and reducing it as learning progresses. Positive action probabilities help exploration; sufficient visits also depend on which states can be reached.

For an episode, compute returns backward with $G\leftarrow R_{t+1}+\gamma G$, update action-value averages according to the selected visit rule, then update the $\epsilon$-greedy policy.

## Lecture 6. Stochastic approximation

### Incremental mean estimation

Let $x_k$ be a new sample and $w_k$ the mean of the first $k-1$ samples. The next estimate is

$$
w_{k+1}=w_k+\frac1k(x_k-w_k).
$$

This replaces repeated full summation with a correction based on the latest prediction error.

### Robbins-Monro root finding

Suppose we want $g(w)=0$ but can observe only $\tilde g(w_k)=g(w_k)+\eta_k$, with observation noise $\eta_k$. Robbins-Monro uses

$$
w_{k+1}=w_k-a_k\tilde g(w_k),\qquad a_k>0.
$$

The Chinese flowchart has four steps: begin with a root estimate, obtain a noisy measurement, correct the estimate using that measurement, and repeat. Its example uses $\tilde g(w_k)=w_k^3-5+\eta_k$, aiming for $\sqrt[3]5\approx1.71$.

![An illustrated convergence path for Robbins-Monro root finding](images/053.png)

The theorem displayed in the notes assumes an increasing function with derivative bounded above and away from zero, appropriate zero-mean noise with controlled variance, and step sizes satisfying

$$
\sum_ka_k=\infty,\qquad\sum_ka_k^2<\infty.
$$

The example $a_k=1/k$ satisfies these summability requirements. The abbreviation **w.p.1** means **with probability one**. The later stochastic-convergence slide uses the same ideas of sufficient cumulative progress, diminishing squared step sizes, and controlled noise.

To recover mean estimation, set $g(w)=w-\mathbb E[X]$ and use the noisy observation $w-x_k$.

### SGD as stochastic root finding

Let $w$ be a parameter vector, $X$ a random data sample, and

$$
J(w)=\mathbb E[f(w,X)].
$$

Under conditions permitting the exchange of expectation and differentiation, the stationary equation is $g(w)=\nabla_wJ(w)=\mathbb E[\nabla_wf(w,X)]=0$. A sampled gradient supplies a noisy measurement, giving

$$
w_{k+1}=w_k-\alpha_k\nabla_w f(w_k,x_k).
$$

Thus SGD is a Robbins-Monro method applied to a gradient root. For a fixed training set, introduce a random variable that selects a training example uniformly; empirical average loss becomes an expectation over that index.

The slides compare relative gradient error with distance from an optimum. Their $\nabla_w^2 f$ denotes a second derivative or Hessian. The mean value theorem connects gradients at different parameter values through curvature; positive lower curvature bounds are an additional assumption in that argument.

Batch gradient descent uses the whole dataset. Mini-batch descent averages a sample of examples. The lecture's mini-batch example samples with replacement: a batch whose size equals the dataset size can contain duplicates. A full pass without replacement is a different sampling scheme.

## Lecture 7. Temporal-difference learning

### TD prediction

Let $V_t$ be the current state-value estimate, $\alpha_t$ a step size, and $(S_t,R_{t+1},S_{t+1})$ an observed transition. Define

$$
Y_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V_t(S_{t+1}),
\qquad \delta_t=Y_t-V_t(S_t).
$$

The TD update is

$$
V_{t+1}(S_t)=V_t(S_t)+\alpha_t\delta_t,
$$

with other table entries unchanged. The target $Y_t$ uses a sampled reward and a bootstrapped estimate of future return. Some slides define the error with the opposite sign and subtract it; the update is equivalent when that sign is used consistently.

For a fixed policy, TD prediction solves the Bellman **expectation** equation. Its residual compares the sampled target with the current estimate. Even at the true value, an individual sampled TD error can be nonzero; the appropriate conditional expectation is zero.

The tabular convergence discussion uses sufficient state visits and per-state step sizes with divergent sums and convergent squared sums, along with the usual fixed-policy sampling assumptions. Constant step sizes are useful for continued adaptation and have different convergence behavior.

### Monte Carlo and TD

| Aspect | Monte Carlo | TD prediction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Target | A completed return $G_t$ | Reward plus a next-state estimate |
| Timing | Wait for the sampled return | Update after each transition |
| Bootstrapping | No | Yes |
| Natural setting | Episodic returns | Episodic and continuing tasks |
| Source of variation | Random outcomes over a whole return | Sampled transition and changing value estimates |

The notes associate long sampled returns with higher variance. TD usually trades some target bias from value approximation for shorter-horizon sampling.

### SARSA, expected SARSA, and multi-step returns

Let $Q_t$ estimate action values and let the observed tuple be $(S_t,A_t,R_{t+1},S_{t+1},A_{t+1})$. SARSA updates

$$
Q_{t+1}(S_t,A_t)=Q_t(S_t,A_t)
+\alpha_t[R_{t+1}+\gamma Q_t(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})-Q_t(S_t,A_t)].
$$

Use these value estimates for policy improvement, for example by updating an $\epsilon$-greedy policy.

Expected SARSA changes the target to

$$
R_{t+1}+\gamma\sum_a\pi(a\mid S_{t+1})Q_t(S_{t+1},a).
$$

An $n$-step SARSA return includes several observed rewards before bootstrapping:

$$
G_t^{(n)}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\gamma^jR_{t+j+1}
+\gamma^nQ_t(S_{t+n},A_{t+n}),
$$

with the terminal bootstrap omitted when the episode ends first. Increasing the return horizon connects one-step bootstrapping to Monte Carlo returns.

### Q-learning and on-policy versus off-policy learning

Q-learning uses the target

$$
Y_t^{Q}=R_{t+1}+\gamma\max_aQ_t(S_{t+1},a).
$$

Its maximization corresponds to the Bellman optimality equation. By comparison, fixed-policy SARSA-style evaluation estimates the expected return under the specified policy.

Distinguish the **behavior policy**, which collects experience, from the **target policy**, which the update evaluates or improves. Matching these policies gives on-policy learning; differing policies give off-policy learning. In common Q-learning control, the behavior policy explores while the target uses a greedy backup.

![An exploratory behavior policy, a sampled grid-world trajectory, and the resulting Q-learning estimate](images/085.png)

The general form is $Q\leftarrow Q+\alpha(\text{target}-Q)$. The target determines which algorithm and which equation are being learned.

## Lecture 8. Value-function approximation

### From tables to functions

Large or continuous state spaces make tables expensive and limit generalization. Let $w$ be a parameter vector and $\phi(s)$ a feature vector. A linear approximation is

$$
\hat v(s,w)=\phi(s)^Tw.
$$

For the source's scalar example, $\phi(s)=[s,1]^T$ and $w=[a,b]^T$, so $\hat v(s,w)=as+b$. The function is linear **in its parameters**, even when features themselves are nonlinear functions of state.

Updating shared parameters changes predictions at multiple states. This generalization is the main advantage illustrated by the curve-fitting example.

### Stationary distributions and weighted squared error

Let $P_\pi$ be the state-transition matrix under a fixed policy. A stationary state distribution $d_\pi$ satisfies

$$
d_\pi^TP_\pi=d_\pi^T,
\qquad d_\pi(s)\geq0,
\qquad\sum_sd_\pi(s)=1.
$$

Under suitable long-run sampling conditions, empirical state-visit frequencies estimate this distribution. It depends on the policy and the environment, rather than on the choice of $\epsilon$-greedy exploration alone.

The objective in the slides is a state-frequency-weighted squared error:

$$
J(w)=\sum_sd_\pi(s)(v_\pi(s)-\hat v(s,w))^2.
$$

Its gradient is

$$
\nabla_wJ(w)=-2\mathbb E_{S\sim d_\pi}
[(v_\pi(S)-\hat v(S,w))\nabla_w\hat v(S,w)].
$$

Since the true value is unknown, use a sampled return or a TD target. Absorbing constant factors into the step size gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{MC:}\quad
w_{t+1}&=w_t+\alpha_t(G_t-\hat v(S_t,w_t))\nabla_w\hat v(S_t,w_t),\\
\text{TD:}\quad
w_{t+1}&=w_t+\alpha_t\delta_t\nabla_w\hat v(S_t,w_t),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma\hat v(S_{t+1},w_t)-\hat v(S_t,w_t)$. In this TD update, the target is treated as fixed when differentiating, giving a semi-gradient update.

### Deep Q-learning: main network, target network, and replay

Let $Q_w(s,a)$ be a neural action-value approximation. Its trainable parameters are $w$. A network can represent a state-action mapping; a common discrete-action implementation takes the state and returns values for all available actions.

Keep target parameters $w_T$ fixed while computing

$$
y_T=r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q_{w_T}(s',a'),
\qquad
L(w)=\mathbb E[(y_T-Q_w(s,a))^2].
$$

Use $y_T=r$ for a terminal transition. Initialize the two networks from the same parameters, update the main network repeatedly, and periodically copy its parameters into the target network.

**Experience replay** stores transitions and samples old experience again. Random sampling reduces correlations between successive updates and improves data reuse. Uniform replay gives each stored transition equal sampling probability. The target network addresses the separate problem of a rapidly moving learning target. Replay can also be used with tabular methods; uniform replay is the particular scheme discussed here.

The source algorithm is:

1. Store transitions from the behavior policy in a replay buffer.
2. Sample a mini-batch.
3. Compute targets with the fixed target network.
4. Update the main network by squared prediction error.
5. Refresh the target network at the chosen interval.

## Lecture 9. Policy gradients

### A parameterized policy needs a performance objective

Let $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ be a policy parameterized by vector $\theta$. Forward propagation computes action probabilities or distribution parameters; gradient-based updates change the policy.

For a state weighting $d$, an average-value objective is $J(\theta)=\sum_sd(s)v_{\pi_\theta}(s)$. If $d$ depends on the policy, that dependence participates in the objective's derivative. The policy-gradient theorem organizes the resulting gradient using the state weighting appropriate to the selected objective.

The lecture also considers stationary average reward. With $r_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)r(s,a)$, define

$$
\bar v_\pi=\sum_sd_\pi(s)v_\pi(s),
\qquad \bar r_\pi=\sum_sd_\pi(s)r_\pi(s).
$$

For a stationary distribution and discounted values, multiplying the Bellman equation by $d_\pi^T$ gives $\bar r_\pi=(1-\gamma)\bar v_\pi$. This is the relationship used in the original metric comparison.

### The log-probability trick

For an action with positive policy probability,

$$
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)
=\frac{\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}.
$$

A policy-gradient expression can therefore be written as an expectation of a log-probability gradient times action value. For example, for a normalized discounted objective and its matching occupancy distribution $d_{\pi_\theta}$,

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{S\sim d_{\pi_\theta},\,A\sim\pi_\theta}
[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A\mid S)q_{\pi_\theta}(S,A)].
$$

An unnormalized discounted objective introduces the corresponding constant scale. The important computational structure is the expectation of **score times return or value**.

Softmax converts finite action scores into strictly positive probabilities that sum to one. To obtain a usable update, replace the expectation with samples and replace the unknown action value with an estimate.

### Monte Carlo policy updates

Generate episodes with the current policy, compute returns, and perform stochastic gradient ascent:

$$
\theta\leftarrow\theta+\beta_t
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)\hat q_t.
$$

Here $\beta_t$ is the policy learning rate and $\hat q_t$ may be the sampled return $G_t$, with discount weighting matched to the chosen objective. The function being updated is the policy, rather than a table of action values. Complete-return MC updates wait until the return is available; this is episode-based learning, distinct from training solely on a fixed offline dataset.

## Lecture 10. Actor-critic methods

### The two roles

The **actor** updates a parameterized policy. The **critic** estimates its values and supplies an evaluation signal. Actor-critic methods retain the policy-gradient structure while learning the value estimate used in the update.

For an action-value critic $Q_w$, a SARSA-style TD update is

$$
w\leftarrow w+\alpha_w
[R_{t+1}+\gamma Q_w(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})-Q_w(S_t,A_t)]
\nabla_wQ_w(S_t,A_t).
$$

The actor uses the critic's estimate:

$$
\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha_\theta
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)Q_w(S_t,A_t).
$$

### Baselines and advantage

A state-dependent baseline $b(s)$ can be subtracted without changing the expected policy-gradient contribution at a fixed state:

$$
\mathbb E_{A\sim\pi_\theta}
[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A\mid s)b(s)]
=b(s)\nabla_\theta\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=0.
$$

The source's variance-minimizing scalar baseline at state $s$ weights action values by the squared score norm:

$$
b^*(s)=\frac{\mathbb E_{A\sim\pi_\theta}
[\|\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A\mid s)\|^2q_\pi(s,A)]}
{\mathbb E_{A\sim\pi_\theta}[\|\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A\mid s)\|^2]}.
$$

Ignoring those score-norm weights motivates the convenient baseline $b(s)=v_\pi(s)$. The Chinese value diagram states that $v_\pi(s)$ is the policy-weighted average of $q_\pi(s,a)$. Define advantage by

$$
A_\pi(s,a)=q_\pi(s,a)-v_\pi(s).
$$

Positive advantage means that action is better than the policy's average action at the same state. Some original slides write this advantage as $\delta_\pi(s,a)$; this edition reserves $\delta_t$ for a sampled TD error.

### A state-value critic and sampled advantages

Let $V_w$ estimate state value and define

$$
\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V_w(S_{t+1})-V_w(S_t).
$$

Using the true $v_\pi$ and subsequent behavior following $\pi$ gives $\mathbb E[\delta_t\mid S_t=s,A_t=a]=A_\pi(s,a)$. Thus the TD error supplies a sampled advantage estimate. A state-value critic can avoid maintaining a separate action-value network in this update:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w&\leftarrow w+\alpha_w\delta_t\nabla_wV_w(S_t),\\
\theta&\leftarrow\theta+\alpha_\theta\delta_t
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

### Importance sampling and off-policy actor-critic

Let $p_0$ be a target distribution and $p_1$ a sampling distribution that covers its support. Then

$$
\mathbb E_{X\sim p_0}[f(X)]
=\mathbb E_{X\sim p_1}\left[\frac{p_0(X)}{p_1(X)}f(X)\right].
$$

The Chinese question panel asks why sampling is useful when the probability function is already known. Evaluating a probability at a supplied point can be easy while summing or integrating a high-dimensional expectation remains difficult. Samples provide a computational route to that expectation.

For behavior policy $\beta$ and target policy $\pi_\theta$, the action importance ratio is

$$
\rho_t=\frac{\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)}{\beta(A_t\mid S_t)}.
$$

$A_t\sim\beta(\cdot\mid S_t)$ means the collected action came from the behavior policy. The target policy remains the one being optimized. At a fixed state, this ratio corrects the action distribution.

The lecture's actor update has the form

$$
\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha_\theta\rho_t
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)\hat A_t.
$$

Its accompanying state-value critic uses an importance-weighted TD update, $w\leftarrow w+\alpha_w\rho_t\delta_t\nabla_wV_w(S_t)$. The chosen objective and sampling scheme determine how state-distribution differences and off-policy value estimation must also be handled; an action ratio alone addresses the conditional action distribution.

### Deterministic policies and DDPG

A stochastic policy can represent continuous actions with a probability density, such as a Gaussian. A deterministic policy instead maps a state directly to an action: $a=\mu_\theta(s)$. Exploration for such an actor can be supplied by noise or a separate behavior mechanism.

The deterministic policy-gradient expression uses an occupancy weighting $\rho_\mu$:

$$
\nabla_\theta J
=\mathbb E_{S\sim\rho_\mu}
\left[\nabla_\theta\mu_\theta(S)
\left.\nabla_aq_\mu(S,a)\right|_{a=\mu_\theta(S)}\right],
$$

with the objective's normalization understood. The Chinese chain-rule diagram reads:

**How parameters change the action, multiplied by how the action changes value, determines how parameters change performance.**

The two Chinese network diagrams distinguish the interfaces:

| Component | Inputs | Output | Role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Actor $\mu_\theta$ | State, such as position and velocity | An action, such as steering value $0.3$ | Choose what to do |
| Critic $Q_w$ | State and candidate action | Estimated cumulative return, such as $8$ | Evaluate that choice |

DPG means deterministic policy gradient. DDPG uses deep neural networks for the actor and critic. Off-policy status follows from the relationship between behavior and target policies; deterministic action selection alone does not decide that relationship.
